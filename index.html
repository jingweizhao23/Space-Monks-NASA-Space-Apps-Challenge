    How Analog Atlas works

    Before astronauts and robots go to the Moon or Mars, teams practise on Earth in places that look and feel similar. These places are called analogs. Analog Atlas helps you find the best one for a job.

    Using it in four steps

    1. Pick a target on the Moon or Mars in the top-left box.
    2. Pick what you want to test: landing, rover driving, finding resources, living there, or spacewalk practice. Each one has its own rules and weights.
    3. See the best match. It's starred at the top of the Top matches list (bottom left), and a pulsing gold ring marks it on the globe. Every other dot is a place on Earth: brighter yellow means a closer match, and a white ring means space agencies already use it.
    4. Click a dot to fly there and see its details, the reasons it matches, and a 3D model of the area.

    The match score (0–100)

    Each purpose works in three steps. 1. Filters: some purposes have hard NASA limits, for example a lander needs ground under 10° (Moon) or 5° (Mars). A place that fails gets no score and shows as a grey dot. 2. Feature matches: each feature gets a 0–1 match. Numbers score by how close they are (1 − difference ÷ tolerance); categories score 1 for the same type, 0.5 for a related type, 0 otherwise. 3. Weighted total: the matches are added using that purpose's weights, out of 100.

    Some Moon and Mars values are beyond anything on Earth (no Earth place swings 200 °C between day and night, or stays sunlit 86% of the year). For those, we score against Earth's own range: the Earth places closest to the target score highest. Those values are tagged off-Earth.

    The colours on the globe are stretched across the current scores, so they always show which places are relatively better.

    The rankings (bottom left)

    Top matches ranks every place and says in plain words why #1 wins. New ideas lists the best places nobody uses yet. Reality check runs two tests on our own score. First: places that space agencies really use for this purpose (★) should rank high; where they don't, we say why. Second: we move each weight by ±25% and count how many of the top 10 stay the same, to show the answer doesn't hinge on our exact weights.

    The 3D view (bottom right)

    This shows the real shape of the land around the site, using elevation data from the globe (Cesium World Terrain, mostly ~30 m satellite elevation models), with heights stretched so the relief is easy to see; the label says by how much. The dashed square is the 2 km box the steepness and roughness numbers are measured in. The ground colours, frost, ice and dust are illustrations of the feature values, not satellite imagery. Drag to spin it and scroll to zoom. The sliders below it let you ask "what if": change one feature, such as steeper ground, more ground ice or a bigger temperature swing, and watch the land and the match score change. Make it like the target shows what the place would need to become.

    Moving around the globe

    Scroll to zoom, left-drag to move, right-drag to tilt and rotate. The buttons at the top right show or hide each panel and switch between light and dark mode.

    Where the data comes from

    Earth, measured (500 places): steepness, share of gentle ground and roughness come from Cesium World Terrain, sampled every 100 m over a 2 km box around each point. Temperatures, temperature swings, wind and cloudiness come from NASA POWER (2001–2025 averages, ~50 km cells). POWER gives the temperature at each cell's average height, so we adjust it to the site's own height (−6.5 °C per km); without this a mountain top like Mauna Kea would read 21 °C instead of about −2 °C. Illumination = daylight hours for the latitude × sunlight getting through clouds.

    Earth, from geologic maps (tagged map): the rock type at each point is read from published geologic maps through the Macrostrat map service (29 maps, from USGS state maps to the Geological Map of Africa). Where a map names a specific rock we use it directly and work out the likely minerals from it. Where the map is only broad (for example "sedimentary"), we keep our regional class if it is in the same rock family (map ✓). Our first regional guesses agreed with the maps at family level about 74% of the time.

    Earth, estimated (tagged est.): surface material (loose / mixed / bedrock) for most points, minerals where the map is too broad, and ground ice, which is estimated from mean air temperature. Next step: NASA EMIT minerals and ESA Permafrost CCI.

    Moon and Mars targets: values from published mission results (LRO Diviner and LOLA, Perseverance MEDA, MRO CRISM, SWIM ice maps). Hover a target value in the details table to see its source. Several are estimates and are marked as such in data/targets.json.

    The live check box calls NASA POWER again each time you click a site, so you can confirm the stored numbers.

    What no place on Earth can copy

    Lower gravity, airless or thin-air conditions, space radiation and, on Mars, toxic soil. Every site lists these so nobody mistakes a good analog for the real thing.
